Implied volatility (IV) explained simply

Implied volatility (IV) is the market's estimate of how much a stock or index is likely to move in the future, worked backwards from option prices. When traders are willing to pay more for options, IV rises. When options get cheaper, IV falls. It is the single biggest reason why two options with the same strike and expiry can cost very different amounts at different times.

Where IV comes from

An option pricing model, such as Black-Scholes, takes a few inputs: the current price, the strike, time to expiry, interest rates and volatility. Everything except volatility is known. So traders do the sum in reverse: they plug in the option's actual market price and solve for the volatility that would produce it. That solved number is the implied volatility, quoted as an annualised percentage.

What an IV number means

An IV of 16% on an index means the options are priced as if the index will move about 16% over a year, one standard deviation, which is roughly a two-in-three probability range. You can scale it to shorter periods by dividing by the square root of time. For a single trading day, divide by about 16 (the square root of roughly 252 trading days), so 16% IV implies a typical daily move of about 1%. This is an approximation the market is pricing in, not a promise.

Implied vs historical volatility

  • Historical (realised) volatility measures how much the price actually moved in the past.
  • Implied volatility measures how much the options market expects it to move in the future.

When IV is well above recent realised volatility, options are "expensive" relative to how the stock has behaved. When IV is below realised volatility, they are relatively cheap. Option sellers and buyers both watch this gap.

IV rank and IV percentile

A raw IV of 30% can be high for one stock and low for another. To compare, traders use:

  • IV rank: where today's IV sits between its lowest and highest level over the past year, from 0 to 100.
  • IV percentile: the percentage of days in the past year when IV was lower than today.

Both tell you whether options are cheap or expensive compared with that stock's own history. The IV screener on Vayron ranks F&O stocks this way.

IV crush

Before known events, such as quarterly results, a central bank policy decision or a budget, uncertainty is high and IV usually rises. Once the event passes, uncertainty drops and IV often falls sharply. This is called IV crush. A buyer can be right about the direction and still lose money if the move is smaller than the market had priced in, because the fall in IV shrinks the option's value. Sellers who wrote options before the event benefit from the crush, but they carry the risk of a large surprise move.

The volatility smile and skew

IV is usually not the same at every strike. In Indian index options, OTM puts often carry higher IV than OTM calls because investors pay up for crash protection. Plotting IV across strikes gives a curve known as the smile or skew. A steepening put skew means demand for downside protection is rising.

How IV links to the Greeks and VIX

An option's sensitivity to IV is called vega, covered in the option Greeks lesson. For the whole market, India VIX is essentially a blended implied volatility of NIFTY options, so it moves with index IV.

Frequently asked questions

Is high implied volatility good or bad?

It is neither. High IV makes options expensive, which favours sellers but means larger expected moves and risk. Low IV makes options cheap for buyers but signals the market expects calm.

What is IV crush?

IV crush is the sharp drop in implied volatility after an expected event such as results or a policy decision. It can cause option prices to fall even if the underlying moves in the buyer's favour.

How is IV related to India VIX?

India VIX is calculated from NIFTY option prices and represents the market's expected 30-day volatility, so it is closely tied to the implied volatility of NIFTY options.
