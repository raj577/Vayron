The Fear and Greed Index explained

Markets are driven by people, and people swing between fear and greed. A Fear and Greed Index tries to measure that mood in a single number, usually on a scale from 0 (extreme fear) to 100 (extreme greed). Several versions exist; the best known are one for US stocks and one for crypto.

How the crypto Fear and Greed Index works

The widely followed crypto version, published by alternative.me, focuses on Bitcoin and the wider crypto market. It combines several inputs, each scored and weighted:

  • Volatility: current volatility and drawdowns compared with recent averages. Unusually high volatility is read as fear.
  • Market momentum and volume: current volume and momentum compared with recent averages. Strong buying volume is read as greed.
  • Social media: the pace and tone of crypto-related posts and interactions.
  • Bitcoin dominance: Bitcoin's share of the total crypto market. Rising dominance often means money is retreating to the largest coin, a sign of caution; falling dominance can mean speculative appetite for smaller coins.
  • Search trends: changes in search interest for crypto terms.

The publisher has listed surveys as another input in the past. Methods can change, so check the source for current details.

The stock market version

For US equities, a popular Fear and Greed Index combines indicators such as market momentum versus moving averages, the number of stocks at new highs versus new lows, market breadth, the put-call ratio, junk bond demand, the VIX volatility index and safe-haven demand. In India there is no single official equivalent, but traders build a similar picture from India VIX, the put-call ratio, breadth and money flow.

Reading the number

  • Low readings (roughly below 25): extreme fear. Prices have often fallen sharply and pessimism is widespread.
  • Middle readings (around 50): neutral.
  • High readings (roughly above 75): extreme greed. Prices have usually risen quickly and optimism is high.

How traders use it

The most common use is contrarian. The old saying, popularised by Warren Buffett, is to be "fearful when others are greedy and greedy when others are fearful". Extreme fear has often appeared near market lows, and extreme greed near overheated highs. In practice:

  • Long-term investors may treat extreme fear as a reminder not to panic-sell.
  • Short-term traders may treat extreme greed as a reason to tighten stops or reduce leverage.
  • Some combine it with funding rates and open interest to see whether leverage is building up.

Limitations

  • Extremes can last. The index can stay in "extreme greed" for weeks during a strong bull market, or in "extreme fear" through a long decline.
  • It is a summary of past data. It describes the mood that has already formed, not what will happen next.
  • It is not a timing tool. It gives context, not entry or exit points.
  • Different versions measure different things. A crypto reading says little about Indian stocks.

A balanced way to use sentiment

  1. Note the reading and how fast it is changing.
  2. Compare it with price trend and volatility.
  3. Ask whether your own decisions are being driven by the same fear or greed.
  4. Stick to your plan and position size from the risk management lesson.

Frequently asked questions

What does extreme fear mean in the Fear and Greed Index?

It means sentiment indicators such as volatility, momentum and social activity point to widespread pessimism. It has often appeared after sharp falls, but it does not guarantee a bottom.

Is the Fear and Greed Index a buy or sell signal?

No. It summarises market mood and is best used as context alongside price, volatility and your own risk plan.

Is there a Fear and Greed Index for the Indian market?

There is no single official one. Traders build a similar view from India VIX, the put-call ratio, breadth and money flow.
