Futures basis, premium and discount explained

A futures contract lets you buy or sell an index or stock at a fixed price on a future date. The futures price is rarely exactly equal to the current (spot) price. The gap between them is called the basis, and it carries useful information about financing costs, dividends and positioning.

The definition

Basis = futures price âˆ’ spot price. Some traders flip the sign, so always check which convention a screen uses. On Vayron, a positive basis means the future trades above spot.

  • Premium: the future is above spot. This is the normal state.
  • Discount: the future is below spot. This is less common and worth noticing.

Why futures usually trade at a premium

Holding a future instead of the actual shares lets you keep your cash invested elsewhere until expiry. To keep things fair, the futures price includes that financing cost. This is known as cost of carry. A simplified fair-value formula is:

So with more days left and higher interest rates, the fair premium is larger. Expected dividends reduce it, because the future holder does not receive the dividend that a shareholder would.

Convergence at expiry

As expiry approaches, the cost of carry shrinks to zero, so the basis narrows. On expiry the futures settle at the closing price of the underlying, so basis converges to roughly zero. A premium that is wide early in the month and steadily shrinks is completely normal.

What unusual basis can tell you

  • Premium well above fair value: strong demand to be long through futures. Traders sometimes read this as aggressive bullish positioning.
  • Discount to spot: heavy selling or hedging in futures, a large expected dividend, or constraints on borrowing stock. It often appears during sharp sell-offs or around record dates.
  • Sudden change in basis with rising OI: combine with the build-up labels from the open interest lesson. Rising premium with rising OI fits a long build-up; a shrinking premium or discount with rising OI fits a short build-up.

Arbitrage keeps it in check

If the premium becomes too large, arbitrage desks can buy the shares and sell the future to lock in the gap, known as cash-and-carry arbitrage. If the future trades at too deep a discount, they can do the reverse where possible. This activity usually keeps index basis close to fair value, which is why large deviations stand out.

Rollover

Near expiry, traders who want to keep a position close the current-month future and open the next month's. The rollover percentage is the share of open interest carried into the next series, and the roll cost is the spread between the two months. High rollovers with a rising roll cost suggest traders are keen to keep long positions; high rollovers with a falling roll cost can point to shorts being carried.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the NIFTY future higher than the spot price?

Because of cost of carry. A futures buyer does not pay the full value upfront, so the futures price includes the interest cost of holding the position until expiry, minus expected dividends.

What does it mean when a stock future trades at a discount?

It can reflect heavy selling or hedging in futures, an upcoming dividend, or limits on borrowing shares. It is unusual enough that traders check the reason.

What happens to the basis on expiry day?

It converges towards zero, because futures settle at the closing price of the underlying on expiry.
