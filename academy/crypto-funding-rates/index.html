Most crypto derivatives trading happens in perpetual futures, often called "perps". Unlike NSE futures, perps have no expiry date. To keep their price close to the actual spot price of the coin, exchanges use a regular payment between traders called the funding rate. It is one of the most watched sentiment indicators in crypto.

Why perpetual futures need funding

A normal future converges to spot at expiry, as explained in the futures basis lesson. A perpetual never expires, so something else has to pull it back towards spot. Funding does this by making the crowded side pay the other side:

  • When the perp trades above spot, funding is usually positive and longs pay shorts. This makes holding longs more expensive and encourages shorts, pushing the price back down.
  • When the perp trades below spot, funding is usually negative and shorts pay longs.

The payment is between traders, not to the exchange, and it is based on the size of your position, not your margin.

How often it is paid

Many large exchanges settle funding every 8 hours, though some contracts use 4-hour or 1-hour intervals, and the schedule can change during volatile markets. You only pay or receive funding if you hold a position at the funding timestamp.

How the rate is set

Exchanges typically calculate funding from two parts: an interest component, a small fixed amount reflecting the different interest rates of the two currencies, and a premium component that measures how far the perp has traded from the spot index over the period. A common baseline is 0.01% per 8 hours when the market is balanced. The exact formula, caps and floors differ by exchange.

What funding tells you about sentiment

  • Moderately positive funding: normal in a rising market; more traders want to be long.
  • Very high positive funding: longs are crowded and paying heavily. If price stalls, leveraged longs can be forced out, causing sharp drops known as long liquidations.
  • Negative funding: shorts are crowded. A rise can force shorts to buy back, sometimes causing a short squeeze.
  • Funding diverging from price: rising price with falling funding can suggest spot buying rather than leveraged speculation.

Extreme funding is often read as a contrarian warning, but crowded trades can stay crowded for a long time.

Funding, open interest and liquidations together

Traders combine funding with open interest and liquidation data. Rising OI with high positive funding means more leveraged longs are piling in. A sudden drop in OI with a burst of liquidations shows those positions being flushed out. Market-wide mood indicators like the Fear & Greed Index add another view.

Important to know

  • Crypto markets trade 24/7 and are far more volatile than most stocks.
  • Crypto assets and offshore crypto exchanges are not regulated by SEBI, and India taxes crypto gains differently from shares.
  • Leverage on perps can be very high, so positions can be liquidated quickly. See risk management.

Frequently asked questions

What does a positive funding rate mean?

It means the perpetual future is trading above spot and traders holding long positions pay those holding shorts at each funding interval.

How often are funding rates paid?

Many major exchanges pay funding every 8 hours, but some contracts use 4-hour or 1-hour intervals. You only pay or receive it if you hold a position at the funding time.

Are high funding rates bearish?

Very high positive funding shows crowded, leveraged longs, which can lead to sharp drops if prices stall. Many traders read it as a warning sign, but it is not a precise timing signal.
