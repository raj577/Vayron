Put-call ratio (PCR) explained

The put-call ratio (PCR) compresses a whole option chain into a single number. It compares how many puts are open or traded with how many calls are open or traded. Traders use it as a quick sentiment gauge for indices such as NIFTY and BANKNIFTY.

The formula

There are two common versions:

  • OI PCR = total put open interest Ã· total call open interest, for one expiry.
  • Volume PCR = total put volume traded Ã· total call volume traded, for the day.

If an expiry has 1.2 crore puts open and 1.0 crore calls open, OI PCR is 1.2. OI PCR moves slowly because it reflects positions carried over time. Volume PCR can swing sharply within a single session.

How traders interpret PCR

In the Indian index options market, most open interest is created by option writers (sellers), and writers are often large, well-capitalised participants. That leads to a common reading:

  • Rising PCR (more puts than calls being added) is often read as writers being comfortable selling puts, meaning they expect support below the price. Many traders see this as a supportive or bullish tilt.
  • Falling PCR (more calls being written) is often read as writers expecting a cap above the price, a bearish or range-bound tilt.

There is also a contrarian view. When PCR becomes extremely high, everyone may already be positioned for a rise, and when it becomes extremely low, pessimism may be overdone. At extremes, some traders expect a reversal rather than a continuation.

Because both readings exist, PCR works best as a description of positioning rather than a buy or sell signal. The most useful question is usually "how is PCR changing compared with its own recent range?", not "is it above or below 1?".

Why there is no universal "good" level

Every index and expiry has its own typical range. Hedging demand for puts, the share of weekly versus monthly contracts, and how close the expiry is all shift the baseline. A reading that is high for one index can be ordinary for another. Compare PCR with the same instrument's history, and watch the direction of change during the day.

PCR by strike

Some tools also show PCR at each strike: put OI Ã· call OI at that strike. A strike far above 1 has much more put writing than call writing and is often read as a support area. A strike well below 1 is often read as resistance. This is the same idea as the OI walls described in how to read an option chain.

A worked example

Imagine NIFTY opens with OI PCR at 0.85. Over the morning the index rises, put writers add positions at the strikes just below the price, and some call writers cover. By lunch PCR is 1.05. A trader would describe this as writers shifting support higher. If the index then stalls and call OI builds sharply at the next strike up, PCR may flatten, a sign that the move is meeting supply. These are made-up numbers to show the reasoning.

Limitations

  • PCR counts contracts, not money. Deep OTM puts bought cheaply as insurance count the same as near-the-money puts.
  • Hedgers buy puts to protect stock portfolios, which can lift PCR without any directional view.
  • Near expiry, rolling and settlement distort OI, so PCR can jump around.
  • Large one-off trades can move volume PCR for a few minutes.

Use PCR with other views: price trend, max pain, India VIX and sector money flow.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good put-call ratio?

There is no universal good value. Each index has its own normal range, so it is more useful to compare PCR with its own recent history and to watch which way it is moving.

Is a PCR above 1 bullish?

It is often read as supportive, because put writers are more active than call writers. At very high extremes, though, some traders read it contrarily as crowded optimism.

What is the difference between OI PCR and volume PCR?

OI PCR uses open positions and changes slowly. Volume PCR uses contracts traded today and can swing quickly within a session.
