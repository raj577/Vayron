What is open interest (OI)? A beginner's guide

Open interest, usually shortened to OI, is one of the first numbers you see on any futures or options screen. It sounds technical, but the idea is simple: open interest is the number of contracts that are still open at a given moment. Every open contract has one buyer and one seller, and OI counts how many of those pairs have not yet been closed, squared off or settled.

How open interest goes up and down

OI only changes when positions are opened or closed, not every time a contract changes hands. Four things can happen in a trade:

  • New buyer meets new seller: a fresh contract is created, so OI rises by one.
  • Existing buyer sells to a new buyer: the position just moves from one person to another, so OI stays the same.
  • Existing seller buys back from a new seller: again the position moves, so OI stays the same.
  • Existing buyer and existing seller both close: the contract disappears, so OI falls by one.

That is why OI is often described as a measure of participation and commitment. Rising OI means fresh money is entering positions. Falling OI means traders are closing out and money is leaving.

Open interest vs volume

Volume counts every contract traded during the day, including positions opened and closed within minutes. Open interest counts only what is still standing. A contract can trade ten times in a day and add ten to volume while adding just one, or zero, to OI. High volume with flat OI usually means intraday churn. Rising OI with steady volume usually means positions are being built and carried.

Reading price and OI together

OI on its own does not tell you direction, because every open contract has both a long and a short side. Traders combine it with the price change to describe what kind of positions are being built. The four classic readings are:

PriceOICommon nameWhat it suggests
UpUpLong build-upNew buyers are willing to pay higher prices
DownUpShort build-upNew sellers are pressing prices lower
UpDownShort coveringExisting shorts are buying back to exit
DownDownLong unwindingExisting longs are selling to exit

These labels are a shorthand, not a law. A move driven by short covering can fade once the covering is done, while a long build-up can reverse if news changes. Treat the labels as a description of what has happened so far, not a forecast.

Open interest in options

In options, OI is tracked separately for every strike price and for calls and puts. A strike with very high call OI means many call contracts are open there, and option writers (sellers) often defend those levels. That is why traders talk about OI "walls": large call OI above the price is often read as resistance, and large put OI below the price as support. The option chain lesson explains how to find these strikes, and the put-call ratio and max pain lessons show two popular ways to summarise OI across all strikes.

Change in OI

Most screens also show change in OI, which compares current OI with the previous day's close. This is often more useful than the total, because it shows where positions are being added or removed today. A strike that suddenly adds a lot of put OI during a fall, for example, tells you writers are stepping in at that level.

Limitations to keep in mind

  • OI updates in snapshots during the session, so it lags the live price a little.
  • You cannot see who holds the positions. Hedgers, arbitrageurs and speculators all add to the same number.
  • Near expiry, OI naturally falls as contracts are closed or rolled to the next month, so a drop then is not always a signal.

Frequently asked questions

Is high open interest bullish or bearish?

Neither on its own. Every open contract has a buyer and a seller, so OI measures participation, not direction. You need the price change alongside it to tell long build-up from short build-up.

What is the difference between open interest and volume?

Volume counts every contract traded during the day. Open interest counts only the contracts still open at the end of a period. Volume resets each day, while OI carries over until positions are closed or expire.

Why does open interest fall near expiry?

Traders close positions or roll them into the next expiry, and on expiry day all remaining contracts settle. So falling OI in the last days of a series is normal and not always a signal.
