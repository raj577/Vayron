The Relative Strength Index (RSI) is a momentum indicator created by J. Welles Wilder in 1978. It measures how strong recent gains have been compared with recent losses and scales the result between 0 and 100. It is one of the most widely used indicators on Indian charts and screeners.

How RSI is calculated

  1. Pick a lookback, usually 14 periods (14 candles on whatever timeframe you use).
  2. Work out the average gain on up periods and the average loss on down periods, using Wilder's smoothing.
  3. Divide the two to get the relative strength: RS = average gain Ã· average loss.
  4. Convert it to an index: RSI = 100 âˆ’ 100 Ã· (1 + RS).

If gains and losses are equal, RS is 1 and RSI is 50. If there have been only gains, RSI approaches 100. If only losses, it approaches 0.

Overbought and oversold

The classic levels are 70 and 30:

  • RSI above 70 is called overbought: price has risen quickly and may be stretched.
  • RSI below 30 is called oversold: price has fallen quickly and may be stretched.

"Overbought" does not mean "about to fall". In strong uptrends, RSI can stay above 70 for a long time while price keeps climbing. Many traders therefore treat 70 as a sign of strength in a trend and only look for reversals when other evidence appears. Some adjust the levels to 80/20 for volatile stocks or 60/40 for trend context.

The 50 line

RSI above 50 means average gains are larger than average losses over the lookback, so momentum is positive. Below 50, momentum is negative. Watching whether RSI holds above or below 50 during pullbacks is a simple way to judge trend health.

Divergence

A divergence occurs when price and RSI disagree:

  • Bearish divergence: price makes a higher high but RSI makes a lower high. Momentum is fading even though price is still rising.
  • Bullish divergence: price makes a lower low but RSI makes a higher low. Selling pressure is weakening.

Divergences are warnings, not triggers. They can persist for several swings before anything happens, so traders usually wait for price to confirm, for example by breaking a support or resistance level.

Multi-timeframe RSI

RSI on a 5-minute chart and RSI on a daily chart can tell completely different stories. A stock may be oversold intraday while still in a strong daily uptrend. Checking RSI on several timeframes at once helps:

  • When the higher timeframe is strong (above 50) and the lower timeframe dips to oversold, some traders see a pullback within a trend.
  • When all timeframes are overbought together, the move is stretched across the board.
  • When timeframes conflict, it often signals a choppy, undecided market.

An illustrative example

A stock rallies for six days and daily RSI reaches 76. On the 15-minute chart, RSI has dropped from 80 to 45 as price pulls back. A trend-following trader might wait to see if 15-minute RSI recovers above 50 near a support zone, while a cautious trader notes that daily RSI is stretched and keeps position size small. These numbers are for learning only.

Limitations

  • RSI is derived from price alone, so it lags and adds no new information about volume or flow.
  • In strong trends, overbought and oversold signals fail repeatedly.
  • Settings change results; a 14-period RSI is a convention, not a rule.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good RSI value to buy?

There is no universally good value. Readings below 30 are called oversold and above 70 overbought, but in strong trends RSI can stay at extremes for a long time, so it should be combined with other evidence.

What does RSI 14 mean?

It means the RSI is calculated over the last 14 candles of the chosen timeframe, the default setting Wilder proposed.

What is RSI divergence?

It is when price makes a new high or low but RSI does not, suggesting momentum is weakening. It is a warning sign rather than a trade signal on its own.
